How to read this together

This volume contains the first 42 evenings of the 36 week framework. Read the printed verse, opening question and complete story, ask one or two questions, choose tomorrow’s action, and pray. Source notes are for the parent, not part of the reading.

The scripts contain roughly 520 to 600 words including prompts and prayer. Five to seven minutes is a target. The toddler may repeat a short line, point, move around or leave. Invite your older children to explore the older question after the formal ending when they have more to say.

The same verse returns each night for a week. The family line is a summary, not a Bible quotation. Quotations use the public domain World English Bible, updated Protestant edition. You may use the same reference in your family Bible.

No personal family memories have been invented. Documented accounts fill those places in the framework. The sixth and seventh nights still include fully scripted true accounts, so no preparation or perfect recall is required.

Parent preview, the gentle route, and safety

Preview marked stories before reading. They mention danger, illness, discrimination, captivity, death or persecution without graphic description. A younger child may not be ready. For any marked story that feels too heavy, reread Night 23: There was room for the children. For Noah or Daniel, Night 14: Five loaves and two fish is another complete alternative. Resume the sequence the next evening.

Obey ordinary safe parental instructions, ask when confused, and seek help when an instruction asks for wrong or harm. Parents also listen, lead without humiliation, and apologize. Belonging in the family is not earned by performance.

No one may require a child to accept unsafe touching, make or view a private picture, or keep a secret about threats or harm. Say no when possible, move to safety, and tell a safe adult. Keep telling until someone helps. Fear that prevents resisting does not make a child responsible for abuse. Children do not imitate dangerous rescues. See S01.

The historical people illustrate particular choices. They are not flawless authorities. Canada is honoured through gratitude, service and truthful history. Explicit teaching about the armour of God belongs later in the larger framework; these first weeks build foundations of truth, prayer and ordinary obedience.

Week 1 · Night 01

The boy who got out of bed

True account: 1 Samuel 3:1 to 10

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: When someone calls your name while you are busy, what is the first thing you usually do?

Read aloud

Samuel was lying down in the place where he served God with Eli the priest. It was night. Then Samuel heard his name. He got up and ran to Eli. But Eli had not called him. Eli sent him back to lie down.

It happened again. Samuel heard the call and went to Eli. Again, Eli had not called. Samuel did not yet understand what was happening. The Bible tells us that the Lord’s word had not yet been revealed to him in this way.

When Samuel came the third time, Eli realized that the Lord was calling the boy. He told Samuel how to answer. When God called again, Samuel said, “Speak; for your servant hears.” Those are the words at the centre of our week.

Notice the little movements in this true Bible account. Samuel heard. He got up. He went to someone who could help. He listened to an explanation. Then he answered God. He was learning. Not understanding the first time did not make him a failure.

We are beginning a journey together. We will meet people who carried important messages, cared for friends, served Canada, and did difficult things because they believed something mattered more than their own comfort. Before we reach those stories, we start here: with a listening heart.

A listening heart says that someone else’s words deserve my attention. When Mom or Dad gives an ordinary, safe instruction, you can pause what you are doing, turn toward us, and answer. You do not have to pretend that stopping your game is exciting. You can feel disappointed and still begin to obey.

Parents need listening hearts too. If you are confused, frightened, hurt, or trying to explain something important, we must listen carefully. We do not know everything. God’s Word corrects grownups as well as children.

Samuel’s experience was a special moment in the Bible. You do not need to lie awake tonight waiting to hear a voice. We have Scripture to read together. When we hear what God has said there, we can ask what it means and how to live it. A feeling or an unexpected thought is not automatically a message from God.

Jesus welcomes people who are learning. We can ask him for help when our attention wanders or we have ignored someone. Tonight, our first faithful step is small enough for every person in this room: turn toward God’s Word and be ready to answer.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Touch an ear and say, “We listen.”

Ages five and seven: What did Samuel do when he heard his name?

Ages nine and eleven: How can you show that you are listening even when you would rather keep playing?

Tomorrow: Tomorrow, when Mom or Dad calls, pause safely, turn toward the speaker, and answer respectfully.

Pray: Lord, thank you for speaking through the Bible. Give us hearts ready to listen. Help the children answer, and help the parents listen patiently too. When we misunderstand, help us ask. When we have ignored you, forgive us and help us begin again. Amen.

God’s call to Samuel is a particular biblical event, not an instruction to train children to hear private voices. Respectful attention does not require forced eye contact.

Week 1 · Night 02

The king who needed to listen

True account: 2 Kings 22:8 to 13; 23:1 to 3

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: Would someone who wore a crown still need to be corrected?

Read aloud

Yesterday, a boy listened. Tonight, a king listens.

Josiah became king of Judah when he was a boy. Years later, during work on the Lord’s temple, the priest Hilkiah found the Book of the Law. The book was given to Shaphan, an official, who read it to the king.

What Josiah heard troubled him deeply. God had told his people how to live, but they had not kept his commands. Josiah did not decide that his crown made God’s words unimportant. He sent people to ask about the Lord’s message. Later, he gathered the people and had the words of the covenant read aloud. He committed himself to following the Lord, and the people joined in that commitment.

Think about the order. The king listened before he led. He received correction before he called other people to change. His position did not put him above God.

Samuel had needed Eli’s help to recognize what was happening. Josiah needed the book to show him what was wrong. Both accounts teach us something about our weekly verse. A servant who says, “Speak; for your servant hears,” is willing to hear more than praise. Sometimes God’s Word tells us that we must change direction.

That can happen in an ordinary room like ours. We read that love is patient, then remember how we grabbed a toy. We read that truth matters, then remember the answer we gave to avoid trouble. We read that parents must not provoke their children, then a grownup remembers speaking unfairly.

The right response is to take the correction seriously. We can name what happened, ask forgiveness, and do what can be done to put it right. Being corrected does not mean we have stopped being loved. It means there is something important to learn.

Imagine a parent accidentally gives you the wrong instruction. You are allowed to explain respectfully. A good parent should be willing to check. The family is not a place where the biggest person is always right. It is a place where all of us need God’s truth, and parents carry the responsibility to lead with care.

Josiah’s story also reminds us to open the Bible rather than just assume we remember what it says. During these evenings, we will keep returning to an actual passage. We will not make a hero, a parent, or a country the final judge of right and wrong.

Jesus teaches us truth and offers forgiveness to people who turn to him. We can listen without pretending to be perfect. A crown could not excuse Josiah from listening, and neither can being older, stronger, or certain that we already know.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Open your hands and say, “Teach me.”

Ages five and seven: What did Josiah do when he heard God’s words?

Ages nine and eleven: Why is it important for Mom and Dad to accept correction as well as give it?

Tomorrow: When you receive a fair correction tomorrow, name one thing you can change instead of immediately making an excuse.

Pray: God of truth, help us welcome your correction. Give parents humility and children willing hearts. Show us what needs to change, and give us courage to make it right. Thank you for the forgiveness we can find in Jesus. Amen.

Josiah was older when the book was found. This script does not place that discovery in the first year of his childhood reign.

Week 1 · Night 03

The message that had to arrive

True account: Charles Tomkins and Cree code talkers during the Second World War

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: What could go wrong if an important message arrived with one part missing?

Read aloud

During the Second World War, Canadian service members needed ways to send information without making it easy for opposing forces to understand. Charles Tomkins was a Métis man from Alberta who spoke Cree. That knowledge became an important part of his service.

Tomkins and other Cree speakers worked as code talkers. A message could be translated into Cree before it was sent by radio. A Cree speaker at the receiving end translated it back into English. The people depending on the message needed it to arrive accurately. This work required cooperation between the person speaking and the person listening.

Tomkins was one member of a larger effort. His language was not something he invented for the war. Cree was already a living language, spoken in families and communities. A gift developed among his people became useful in an unexpected way.

Our Bible verse this week is Samuel’s answer to God: “Speak; for your servant hears.” Tomkins’s work is not the same as Samuel’s special calling, but it gives us a picture of why receiving words carefully matters. A message cannot help someone if we do not pay attention to it.

Now bring that picture into our home. Suppose Mom asks you to put your shoes by the door and bring your coat to her. You hear “shoes” but keep thinking about your game. You put away the shoes and forget the coat. You have done part of the job, but you have not received the whole message.

A useful response is to repeat the instruction briefly. “Shoes by the door, then bring my coat.” That is not arguing. It is checking. If you did not hear clearly, ask. Guessing is not more grown up than asking a sensible question.

Parents have work to do here too. We should get your attention and give directions you can understand. A two year old cannot carry the same message as an eleven year old. Different ages need different help. Being patient with a younger brother is one way our family becomes a team.

This Canadian story also teaches us to respect gifts that are different from our own. One person’s language, another person’s careful hearing, and another person’s practical skill can all serve neighbours. We do not need everyone to be identical before we can work together.

Military information sometimes needs protection. That is different from a frightening secret about someone hurting you. In our family, you can always tell a safe adult about harm. Faithful listening helps truth arrive where it belongs. Tomorrow, our task will be much smaller than a wartime message, but we can still receive it carefully and pass it on accurately.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Cup a hand near an ear and say, “Listen carefully.”

Ages five and seven: Why did the person receiving the message need to listen carefully?

Ages nine and eleven: How is checking an instruction different from arguing about it?

Tomorrow: Repeat one instruction back in your own words before you begin it tomorrow.

Pray: Lord, thank you for the languages and abilities you give people. Help us listen carefully and speak accurately. Teach us to ask when we are unsure and to respect people whose gifts are different from ours. Make our family useful to one another. Amen.

Parent note and sources

Veterans Affairs Canada documents the translation method. The Canadian Encyclopedia identifies Tomkins as Métis. No private conversation or religious motive is invented.

Week 1 · Night 04

A place for Mary

True account: Luke 10:38 to 42

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: Who in our family is allowed to learn about God: the oldest, the youngest, the boys, the girl, or everyone?

Read aloud

Jesus came to a village where a woman named Martha welcomed him into her home. Her sister Mary sat at the Lord’s feet and listened to his teaching. Martha was distracted by the work of serving. She came to Jesus with a complaint about her sister leaving the work to her.

Jesus answered Martha with care. He spoke about her worry and distraction, and said that Mary had chosen the good part. Mary’s place as a listener was not going to be taken away from her.

The Bible does not tell us what was on the table or exactly which jobs were waiting. We should not fill in those missing details and then call them facts. It does tell us what mattered in that moment: Mary was listening to Jesus, and Jesus defended her opportunity to learn.

Samuel listened at the place where he served. Josiah listened when a book was read. Mary listened while Jesus taught in a home. God’s words were worth attention in every setting.

Our family includes a sister and four brothers. Every one of you belongs in this learning circle. The oldest child does not exist just to manage the younger children. Boys do not have a special claim on courage or understanding. Girls do not have a special obligation to leave the conversation and do all the serving. Each child can listen, ask, understand, and practise what Jesus teaches.

There will be evenings when the youngest person moves around, makes a sound, or wants to hold something. We can help him join for a short moment without expecting him to behave like an older child. Learning together includes making room for one another.

Does Mary’s story mean you can ignore every chore by saying that you are thinking about God? No. Other Bible passages teach us to serve faithfully. Jesus himself served others. The point here is to receive his teaching and not let worry or comparison push it away. There is time to listen and time to put what we learn into action.

Perhaps you have noticed someone else doing less work than you. That can be frustrating. You may explain it respectfully and ask for help. You do not need to call your brother lazy or your sister selfish. Parents are responsible for arranging the work fairly.

Jesus did not need Mary to earn a seat by being impressive. He welcomed her as a learner. We can come to him that way too: ready to listen, with questions still in our minds and much still to understand.

Tonight, let us make room for every person at the Bible. Tomorrow, we will practise making room for someone else’s words.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Point to yourself and say, “I can learn.”

Ages five and seven: What was Mary doing while Jesus was in the home?

Ages nine and eleven: How can we make sure every child gets to learn without making the oldest child responsible for everyone?

Tomorrow: Let a younger or quieter family member finish one answer without interrupting tomorrow.

Pray: Jesus, thank you for welcoming people to learn from you. Help us listen without comparing ourselves with one another. Give each child room to ask and understand. Help us serve at the right time and receive your teaching with open hearts. Amen.

Parent note and sources

Luke’s account is not a general condemnation of practical service. It protects attention to Jesus amid anxiety and distraction.

Week 1 · Night 05

The word in her hand

True account: Helen Keller and Anne Sullivan, 1887

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: Can someone listen carefully without being able to hear a voice?

Read aloud

Helen Keller was a little girl who could not see or hear. She could learn through touch, but understanding how words named things was still difficult for her. In 1887, a teacher named Anne Sullivan came to help her.

Anne formed letters with her fingers in Helen’s hand. At first, Helen copied some of the movements without understanding how the words worked. Then one day Anne took her to a water pump. Water flowed over one of Helen’s hands while Anne spelled the word for water into the other.

In the account Helen wrote later, she described that moment as the beginning of understanding that the finger movements named the cool water. She wanted to learn more names. The world had become more understandable through words she could feel.

This is Helen’s own remembered account of a real event. It does not mean she suddenly could see or hear. It means she was learning another way to receive language. Patient teaching and a way of communicating that worked for her mattered enormously.

We have been repeating Samuel’s words, “Speak; for your servant hears.” The heart of that answer is willingness to receive God’s message. A person who cannot physically hear can still have a listening heart. Someone with excellent hearing can still refuse to pay attention.

In our home, we do not all learn in exactly the same way. Someone may need a direction repeated. Someone may understand better after seeing what to do. The youngest child may need a grownup to begin the task alongside him. Needing help is not the same thing as being disobedient.

Imagine your little brother cannot work out how to put his shoe on the right foot. Calling him slow will not help. Pointing, showing, and giving him time may help. Imagine your sister explains something you do not understand. You can ask her to show you rather than pretending you know.

Parents must be patient teachers. Children can practise being willing learners. Both responsibilities matter. We are not trying to win a contest about who understands first. We are learning to care for one another while the message becomes clear.

Helen’s story also reminds us not to decide a person’s worth by what is easy or difficult for that person. God made people with dignity. We can honour that dignity by speaking respectfully, offering help without making fun, and noticing abilities we might otherwise overlook.

Tomorrow, when a direction is hard to understand, try a brave little sentence: “Please show me.” When someone else needs that help, answer kindly. A listening family is willing to find a way for the message to be received.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Open a hand and say, “Help me learn.”

Ages five and seven: How did Anne help Helen understand the word for water?

Ages nine and eleven: How can we tell the difference between someone refusing to listen and someone needing a different kind of help?

Tomorrow: Patiently show a younger sibling one simple task, with a parent’s permission.

Pray: Creator God, thank you for every person’s dignity. Help us learn with patience and teach with kindness. Forgive us when we mock someone who needs help. Give us willing hearts and useful ways to understand one another. Amen.

Based on chapter IV of Helen Keller’s 1903 autobiography, with chronology checked against the American Foundation for the Blind. The account is identified as her later recollection.

Can you carry the story?

True account: Review of 1 Samuel 3; 2 Kings 22; Charles Tomkins; Helen Keller

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: Which story began with a voice in the night, and which began to make sense beside a water pump?

Read aloud

Tonight, we will carry the week’s stories from one person to another. The stories are already true. Our job is to remember them carefully, not make them bigger or more exciting.

Start with Samuel. What did he do when he heard his name? Give someone a moment to answer. Here is the account to help us: Samuel went to Eli because he thought Eli had called. After this happened three times, Eli understood that the Lord was calling. Samuel learned to answer God with a servant’s willingness.

Now remember Josiah. He was a king, but hearing God’s Law showed him that the people needed to change. He listened and responded. Being in charge did not put him above God’s words.

Charles Tomkins used Cree as part of wartime communication. A message had to be received and translated carefully. His story helps us remember accuracy. Helen Keller learned the connection between a word formed in her hand and the water flowing over her other hand. Her story helps us remember patience and different ways of learning.

Can someone name the two sisters in the home Jesus visited? They were Martha and Mary. Mary listened to Jesus, and Jesus protected her place as a learner. Everyone in our family needs room at the Bible, including our sister and our youngest brother.

Those are different stories. We should keep their differences. Samuel received a particular call from God. Tomkins received human messages. Helen learned language. Mary listened directly to Jesus. We are not saying that every important instruction is a special message from God. We are noticing what these accounts help us understand about attention, humility, and receiving words.

Our verse brings us back to the centre: “Speak; for your servant hears.” God is worthy of our attention. Reading his Word is a chance to learn, not a competition to look clever.

Now think about a moment this week when you listened well. Perhaps you stopped a game when called. Perhaps you asked for an explanation instead of guessing. Perhaps you waited while a younger person answered. We can thank God for small beginnings.

There may also be something to repair. A parent can go first. A simple admission might be, “I answered before you finished explaining.” A child might remember ignoring a clear instruction. We do not need to give a long speech or blame someone else. We can tell the truth and choose a better next step.

Jesus gives us reason to begin again. Remembering mistakes can lead us toward confession and help, rather than hiding. Tonight, we carry the stories accurately. Tomorrow, we carry one listening habit into an ordinary part of our day.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Touch an ear and repeat, “We listen.”

Ages five and seven: Choose one person from this week and tell us what that person did.

Ages nine and eleven: Which listening habit would make the biggest difference in our home right now?

Tomorrow: Choose one family listening habit to repeat tomorrow. Everyone practises it, including the parents.

Pray: Father, thank you for what we have learned. Help us remember your Word and tell true stories carefully. Forgive our interruptions and ignored instructions. Give us patience to listen, honesty to repair, and joy in learning together. Amen.

Week 1 · Night 07

Jesus stopped to listen

True account: Mark 10:46 to 52

“Speak; for your servant hears.”

1 Samuel 3:10, excerpt · World English Bible

Ask to begin: What does it feel like when you need help and someone tells you to be quiet?

Read aloud

As Jesus was leaving Jericho, a large crowd was with him. A blind man named Bartimaeus was sitting beside the road. When he heard that Jesus was passing, he called out for mercy.

Many people told him to be quiet. Bartimaeus kept calling. Jesus stopped. He told the people to call the man, and Bartimaeus came to him. Jesus asked what he wanted him to do. Bartimaeus wanted to see. Jesus healed him, and Bartimaeus followed him on the road.

We must not turn this account into a promise that every person who prays will immediately be healed. The Bible tells us what Jesus did for this particular man. It also shows us something about the way Jesus treated a person whom others were trying to silence. Jesus gave him attention and invited him to speak.

All week, we have been learning to listen. Samuel answered when called. Josiah accepted correction. Mary received teaching. Tomkins’s work reminds us that messages need care. Helen’s experience reminds us that understanding may need patience. Tonight, Jesus shows us that love notices a voice other people are overlooking.

In our family, obedience does not mean that only parents may speak. Parents should listen to children, and children should learn to listen to one another. Someone being younger does not make that person’s fear or pain unimportant. Someone needing extra time to explain does not make their words worthless.

There is a difference between interrupting because you want the biggest turn and speaking urgently because someone needs help. If a person is hurt or unsafe, tell a grownup right away. You do not need to wait politely while danger continues. When the matter can wait, you can learn to wait without losing your chance to be heard.

Here is a small way we can follow Jesus tomorrow. When a brother or sister starts explaining something, put down what can safely be put down. Let the person finish. You do not have to fix every problem yourself. You may need to bring Mom or Dad. Listening is often the first way we discover what help is needed.

Let us each remember one person who helped us understand something this week. It might be a parent, teacher, sibling, or friend. We can thank God for that person instead of treating their patience as something we are owed.

Our next week will follow Jesus into family life. We will learn that ordinary responsibilities matter to him. First, we close this week remembering that the Lord we are learning to obey is also the Lord who stopped, called a man forward, and listened to his request.

Together: God speaks. We listen.

For the youngest: Hold out an open hand and say, “I hear you.”

Ages five and seven: What did Jesus do when Bartimaeus called for help?

Ages nine and eleven: How can our family make room for important questions while still learning to wait our turn?

Tomorrow: Give one family member your full attention for a short conversation tomorrow.

Pray: Jesus, thank you for hearing people whom others overlook. Help us notice one another’s words and needs. Give us patience for ordinary conversations and courage to speak up about danger. Teach our whole family to listen with love. Amen.

The healing is narrated as the event Mark records, not as a guaranteed outcome for every disability or every prayer.

Week 2 · Night 08

The road back to Nazareth

True account: Luke 2:41 to 52

“And he went down with them and came to Nazareth. He was subject to them, and his mother kept all these sayings in her heart.”

Luke 2:51 · World English Bible

Ask to begin: What important thing could Jesus do in an ordinary family home?

Read aloud

When Jesus was twelve, Mary and Joseph took him to Jerusalem for the Passover feast. When the group began the journey home, they thought he was among the travellers. After a day’s journey, they looked for him among their relatives and acquaintances. He was not there.

They returned to Jerusalem and searched. They found Jesus in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening and asking questions. The people who heard him were amazed at his understanding and answers.

Mary told Jesus that she and Joseph had been searching anxiously. Jesus spoke about being in his Father’s house. Mary and Joseph did not understand what he was telling them. There was something unique about Jesus’ relationship with God that they were still learning to understand.

Then Luke gives us the verse for this week. Jesus went with them to Nazareth and was subject to them. That means he lived under their parental authority. The Son of God returned to the ordinary life of his earthly family.

We are not told a list of the chores Jesus did at twelve. We should not invent one. We are told that he obeyed, and that he continued to grow in wisdom and stature. Family life was part of his real human childhood.

Think about the contrast between the temple and the road home. In the temple, teachers were amazed by his answers. At home, he still honoured Mary and Joseph. Being able to do something impressive did not make ordinary family obedience beneath him.

Sometimes we want the exciting job. Carry the biggest bag. Lead the game. Be first to explain the answer. But then a parent asks us to put a cup on the counter, turn off a screen, or get ready for bed. The small task can feel less important because nobody will applaud it.

Following Jesus includes those small places. When you help family life run well, you are serving real people. Someone does not have to search for your shoes. Someone can finish setting the table. A younger child sees what it looks like to answer respectfully.

Parents remain responsible for giving reasonable, safe instructions and listening when something is unclear. Jesus’ obedience does not make parents perfect. It does show children that being loved and capable is compatible with accepting direction.

Last week we practised receiving words. This week we will practise doing our part. We do not obey to buy a place in our family or make Jesus willing to love us. We are learning to respond to love with faithfulness. Tonight, the road we remember is the road back to Nazareth, where Jesus continued the ordinary life of an obedient son.

Together: Love listens and does its part.

For the youngest: Point to the family and say, “I do my part.”

Ages five and seven: Where did Jesus go after Mary and Joseph found him?

Ages nine and eleven: Which ordinary responsibility are you sometimes tempted to treat as beneath you?

Tomorrow: Choose one routine instruction you will follow tomorrow without waiting for repeated reminders.

Pray: Jesus, thank you for living in a family and showing us faithful obedience. Help us value ordinary responsibilities. Give the children willing hearts and the parents patient wisdom. Forgive us when we treat small duties or one another as unimportant. Amen.

Luke does not provide a childhood chore list or say Jesus had already mastered every human subject. The account affirms both his unique sonship and his growth.

David carried the food first

True account: 1 Samuel 17:12 to 22

“And he went down with them and came to Nazareth. He was subject to them, and his mother kept all these sayings in her heart.”

Luke 2:51 · World English Bible

Ask to begin: Before a person does something famous, what kinds of small responsibilities might need doing?

Read aloud

David is famous for a battle, but tonight we are stopping the story before that battle. We will look at a bag of supplies, some sheep, and an instruction from his father.

David’s three oldest brothers had gone with Saul’s army. David went back and forth between Saul and caring for his father’s sheep at Bethlehem. One day Jesse, his father, gave him a task. He was to take roasted grain and ten loaves of bread to his brothers, and ten cheeses to their commander. Jesse also wanted news of how the brothers were doing.

David got up early. Before leaving, he placed the sheep in the care of a keeper. Then he took the supplies and went as his father had directed. When he reached the camp, he left the things he had brought with the keeper of the supplies and went to greet his brothers.

“We will not hide them from their children, telling to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, his strength, and his wondrous deeds that he has done.”

Psalm 78:4 · World English Bible

Ask to begin: What can happen when many ordinary people decide that a stranger’s life matters?

Read aloud

Hanne Hirsch was a Jewish teenager from Germany. During the Second World War, she was held with family members in the Gurs internment camp in France. In 1941, the Children’s Aid Society, a Jewish aid organization known as OSE, helped get her out and placed her in a children’s home in Le Chambon.

When danger later reached the area, she was hidden on farms. In 1943, she reached Switzerland. Hanne survived and later told about the help she had received.

Her account belonged to a much larger network around Le Chambon and the surrounding villages. Households, schools, religious leaders, Jewish aid workers, and other helpers sheltered people being persecuted. Many local Protestant Christians remembered their own community’s history of persecution. That memory helped shape their willingness to shelter others. People from other beliefs and backgrounds also took part.

Remembering suffering can lead in different directions. A person might decide to care only about their own group. Or that memory can help them recognize someone else’s danger and offer help. In this history, many ordinary people made room for endangered neighbours.

Our verse calls us to pass on the praises and works of God. When we remember what the Bible teaches about mercy and human dignity, the next step should be faithful action. Stories should help us love more wisely, not simply feel impressed for a few minutes.

Think of the connections we can now make. Winton and his partners arranged safety for children. Corrie’s family used a home in the Netherlands. Around Le Chambon, many homes and organizations worked together. These are separate true accounts, joined by the importance of endangered people and practical help.

Our family’s tasks are much smaller and safer. We are not asking children to hide from authorities or carry adult rescue responsibilities. We are learning to notice a person who needs welcome. A new child may not know the rules of a game. A younger sibling may have nobody to sit with. Someone who speaks differently may need patient explanation.

A parent can help you decide how to welcome safely. You do not need to promise secrecy or give up appropriate boundaries. Care works best when it includes truthful communication and responsible adults.

Jesus commands love of neighbour, including people outside our familiar group. Hanne’s account reminds us that the person receiving help has a name and a story. We should remember her, not only celebrate the rescuers.

Tomorrow, ask who might need a small welcome. Learn a name. Explain kindly. Make appropriate room. Let the stories we remember become a faithful action another person can actually experience.
